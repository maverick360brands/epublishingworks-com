**A book description that sells opens with a hook, introduces a character readers want to follow, shows the conflict and what is at stake, signals the genre, and gives readers a reason to start now.**

It may be the most important piece of marketing copy a book ever has. On a retailer page, the cover earns a reader's attention and the description closes the sale. Yet many authors write it last, in a hurry, after spending months or years on the book itself.

This guide breaks down what goes into a strong description, with an example and the most common mistakes to avoid. The same principles apply to back-cover copy on a print edition.

## What is a book description, and how is it different from a synopsis?

A book description, also called a blurb or back-cover copy, is written for readers. Its only job is to make the right reader want the book. It sets up the story and stops before revealing how things turn out.

A synopsis is written for editors, agents, and publishers. It summarizes the entire plot, including the ending, so a professional can judge whether the story works.

Mixing up the two is one of the most common problems in book descriptions. A description that summarizes the whole plot leaves readers no reason to read the book. Keep the synopsis for submissions, which we cover in [how to submit a manuscript to a publisher](/blog/how-to-submit-a-manuscript-to-a-publisher/).

## What are the parts of a book description that sells?

Most effective descriptions follow the same shape, whatever the genre.

### 1. The hook

The first line has one job: getting the reader to read the second line. On many retailer pages, only the first few lines appear before a "Read more" link, so the hook may be all a shopper sees.

Here are a few invented examples of different kinds of hooks:

- **A striking situation:** "The body in the bakery's walk-in freezer was only the second-worst surprise of Maggie's morning."
- **A time, a place, and a promise:** "London, 1815. The war is over, but one officer's battle is just beginning."
- **A central question,** used sparingly: "How do you catch a killer when the only witness is your ex-husband?"

Many retailers let you set the hook in bold, which separates it from the rest of the copy.

### 2. The character and the setup

Introduce the main character with a few specific details: who they are, where they are, and what they want. Readers attach to people, not plots. For a romance, introduce both leads. For a mystery, introduce the sleuth.

Name no more than two or three characters. Every extra name is something a shopper has to remember.

### 3. The conflict and the stakes

What stands in the way, and what happens if the character fails? This is the engine of the description. Specific stakes work better than grand, abstract ones: a reputation, a family business, a second chance at love, a friend accused of murder.

### 4. The genre and tone signals

Readers shop by genre and by mood. A cozy mystery reader wants to know the book is cozy: an amateur sleuth, a close-knit community, some humor, and no graphic violence. A historical romance reader wants the era, the setting, and a sense of the romance's tone. Use the words your readers use and include the details they look for.

### 5. The close

End with a line that makes the next step feel natural. It might restate the stakes, hint at a twist, or tell readers what kind of experience they are in for. Avoid ending on a pile of rhetorical questions.

## What does a strong book description look like?

Here is a made-up example for an imaginary cozy historical mystery, *The Beacon Hill Bookshop Murder*:

> **She came to Boston to sell books, not to solve murders.**
>
> Widowed at thirty, Clara Bell has sunk every cent she has into a struggling bookshop on Beacon Hill. Then her late husband's business partner turns up dead in her stockroom, and the police have only one suspect: Clara.
>
> With help from her sharp-tongued aunt and a bookish police sergeant who seems to know more than he lets on, Clara has one week to find the real killer before she loses her shop, her freedom, and the fresh start she fought so hard for.
>
> The first book in the Clara Bell Mysteries, a cozy historical series set in 1920s Boston. Perfect for readers who love clever amateur sleuths, a vivid period setting, and a mystery with heart.

Here is what each part is doing:

- **The hook** sets up the character's situation and the genre in a single line.
- **The setup** gives Clara a name, a place, a goal, and a problem in two sentences.
- **The stakes** are personal and specific: her shop, her freedom, and her fresh start.
- **The supporting cast** gets roles rather than a list of names.
- **The close** names the series and book number, the era, and the experience readers can expect.

Just as important is what the description leaves out. It does not reveal the killer, summarize the investigation, or explain the backstory in detail.

## How do you write descriptions for a series?

Series readers need a few extra pieces of information:

- **The series name and book number,** in the description and in the retailer's series fields.
- **Whether the book stands alone.** Many readers want to know whether they can start here.
- **What carries over.** Returning characters and settings are a draw for existing fans.

You can see this on our own book pages. Alice Duncan's *Spirits Adopted* is labeled "A Daisy Gumm Majesty Mystery, Book 20," and the note from the publisher tells readers, "You can start anywhere in the series, but you'll want to read all the Daisy Gumm Majesty Mysteries." The same note describes the content plainly, with "no explicit sexual scenes and minimal cursing," and names other authors whose fans will enjoy the series.

A note like that does real work. It answers the questions readers have before they buy: Where do I start? Is this my kind of book? Who else writes like this?

## How should romance, mystery, and historical descriptions differ?

### Romance

Introduce both leads and what keeps them apart. Readers want to feel the emotional conflict and sense the chemistry. Signal the sub-genre and heat level honestly, whether sweet, sensual, or somewhere in between, so the right readers find the book and the wrong ones do not leave disappointed reviews.

### Mystery and crime fiction

Lead with the crime, the sleuth, and the setting. Make both the puzzle and the personal stakes clear. Tone matters a great deal here: a cozy mystery and a gritty police procedural can share a premise and attract completely different readers.

### Historical fiction and historical romance

Put the era and place up front. Our book page for Alice Duncan's *Dancing Angels* sets the scene with "Los Angeles, California, 1927" before the story begins, so readers know exactly where and when they are going.

### Thriller and suspense

Keep it tight and fast. Short sentences, a ticking clock, and a clear threat do more than a long setup.

## How do you format a description for online retailers?

Online descriptions are read on screens, often phones, by people skimming.

- **Front-load the hook.** Assume the first two or three lines are all most shoppers will see.
- **Use short paragraphs** of two to four sentences, with space between them.
- **Use bold sparingly.** A bold hook or tagline helps. A fully bolded description does not.
- **Do not pad it.** Amazon allows up to 4,000 characters, but most strong descriptions use far fewer.
- **Keep reviews and bios separate.** Use the retailer's editorial review and author sections where they exist, so the description stays focused on the story.
- **Use your genre's language naturally.** Phrases like "cozy mystery," "Regency romance," or "small-town" help both readers and retailer search understand the book. Keyword stuffing reads badly and helps no one.

## How is back-cover copy different from an online description?

The same core copy usually serves both, but each has its own limits.

**Back-cover copy** has to share a fixed space with other elements: often a tagline, the description, a review quote or two, a short author line, and room for the barcode and price. That leaves space for a tight version of the description, so cut it to the hook, the setup, and the stakes.

**An online description** has more room and a different reader. Shoppers see it on a retailer page beside the cover, the reviews, and lists of other books, and many read it on a phone. It can carry the series name and number, a short content note, and a line comparing the book to other authors' work.

A practical approach is to write the online version first and then trim it for the back cover. Keep the hook the same in both, so the book makes the same first impression everywhere.

## Who should write your book description?

Many authors find their own description the hardest thing to write, because they know too much about the book. Every subplot feels essential, and every secondary character feels like a friend. A few ways around that:

- **Draft freely, then cut hard.** Write everything you want to say, then strip it back to the five parts above.
- **Ask a reader of your genre** which details made them want to keep reading, and build around those.
- **Work with your publisher.** If your publisher writes the copy, review it for accuracy and tone. You know the book best, and they know how it needs to be presented.

## What mistakes make readers scroll past?

- **Summarizing the whole plot.** The description should open the story, not close it.
- **Opening with backstory.** Start with the situation, not the history that led to it.
- **Too many names.** Stick to the characters readers need to meet.
- **Vague stakes.** "Everything will change" says nothing. Say what will change.
- **Clichés.** Lines like "a heart-stopping tale of love and loss" or "a story you'll never forget" are easy to skip.
- **A mismatch with the cover.** If the cover says lighthearted and the description says dark, readers stop trusting both.
- **Hiding the genre.** A description that is coy about what kind of book it is makes the right readers scroll past.

## How do you refresh the description for a backlist title?

Descriptions written for print editions years ago often do not work online. They may have been written for a different market, squeezed onto a small back cover, or written in a style that now feels dated.

When you reissue an older book:

- **Rewrite from scratch** using the structure above, rather than editing the old copy line by line.
- **Add series information** that may never have appeared on the original edition.
- **Note that the book was previously published,** and under what title if it has changed, so returning readers are not caught by surprise.
- **Bring forward your best review quotes** for the editorial review section.

## How do you test and improve a description?

- **Read it aloud.** Clunky sentences and too many names become obvious.
- **Compare it with bestsellers in your category.** Notice what they include and how quickly they get to the point.
- **Ask readers of your genre,** not just friends and family, whether they would click "buy."
- **Revisit it after reviews arrive.** Readers often tell you what they loved most, and that belongs in the description.
- **Update it when things change,** such as a new book in the series or a new endorsement.
