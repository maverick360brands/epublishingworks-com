**For most genre fiction, and especially for romance, mystery, and backlist series, publish the ebook first, add a paperback for readers who want print, and add an audiobook once the series has shown steady demand.**

That order is a default, not a rule. A memoir for a speaking audience might lead with a paperback. A thriller writer with a large audiobook following might release all three formats on the same day. But for most authors, including most career authors with a backlist, the economics point the same way.

Here is how the formats compare and how to decide.

## How do ebooks, paperbacks, and audiobooks compare?

| | Ebook | Paperback | Audiobook |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cost to produce | Lowest: cover and formatting | Moderate: full cover wrap and print layout | Highest: narration, editing, and mastering |
| Cost per copy sold | None | Printing cost on every copy | None after production |
| Typical price to readers | Lowest | Higher, to cover printing | Often the highest |
| Where readers find it | Online retailers, subscriptions, libraries | Online retailers, bookstores, libraries, events | Audio retailers, subscriptions, libraries |
| Best for | Series readers and worldwide reach | Gifts, events, and readers who prefer print | Commuters, multitaskers, and series listeners |

Each format reaches a different group of readers, and many readers use more than one. The question is not which format is best, but which one to invest in first.

## Why does the ebook usually come first?

### It costs the least to produce

An ebook needs a cover, a properly formatted file, and retail information such as the description, categories, and keywords. There is no printing, shipping, or inventory. A new or reissued title can reach readers without a large upfront investment.

### It reaches readers everywhere at once

Ebook retailers sell around the world. A single release can be available to readers in many countries on the day it goes live.

### Genre readers read heavily in digital

Romance and mystery readers are some of the most voracious readers there are, and many of them read on e-readers, tablets, and phones. A reader who finishes book one of a series at midnight can buy book two in seconds. For series fiction, that instant read-through is powerful.

### It works with subscriptions, preorders, and promotions

- **Subscriptions.** Services such as Kindle Unlimited introduce your books to readers who borrow rather than buy.
- **Preorders.** An ebook can be listed for preorder before release, so readers can commit early and the launch is not your only chance to make an impression.
- **Price promotions.** Ebook promotions, including features through services like BookBub, can bring a series to many new readers at once.

### What does an ebook need?

- **A cover that works as a thumbnail.** Most shoppers first see your cover small, on a screen.
- **A reflowable file,** usually EPUB, so text adjusts to each reader's device and font settings.
- **Clean navigation,** with a linked table of contents and properly marked chapters.
- **Accurate retail information:** title, series name and number, description, categories, and keywords.
- **Back matter that sells the next book,** such as a series list and a preview of the next title.

### How should you price an ebook?

Ebook pricing is flexible, which is another reason to start there. Look at what comparable books in your genre and category sell for, and stay in that range so readers see your book as a fair choice. Many series authors price the first book lower to encourage readers to try the series, then price later books at the genre's usual level. Because ebook prices can be changed quickly, limited-time promotions are easy to run, and they work best when the rest of the series is ready for readers to buy next.

## When should you publish a paperback?

A paperback reaches readers an ebook cannot: people who prefer print, gift buyers, libraries, bookstores, and anyone buying at an event.

### Reasons to add print early

- You speak, teach, or attend events where you can sell copies in person.
- Your readers lean toward print, which is common for some nonfiction, memoir, and children's books.
- You want a physical edition for bookstores and libraries in your community.
- Your book makes a natural gift.

### Print-on-demand vs. offset printing

Most independent paperbacks today use print-on-demand, or POD, which prints each copy when it is ordered. There is no inventory to store, but the cost per copy is higher, and that cost comes out of every sale. Offset printing produces a larger run at a lower cost per copy, but you pay upfront and need somewhere to keep the books. For most genre fiction, print-on-demand is the practical choice.

### What does a paperback need?

- **A full cover wrap:** front, spine, and back. The spine width depends on the page count, so the cover is finished after the interior.
- **A print interior layout:** trim size, margins, fonts, running heads, page numbers, and chapter openings.
- **Its own ISBN.** Each print edition needs a separate ISBN.
- **A printed proof.** Always check a physical copy before release. Problems that are invisible on screen often jump out on paper.

### What about hardcover?

Hardcovers cost more to produce and sell at a higher price. In traditional publishing, many books launch in hardcover first. For independent genre fiction, a hardcover is usually optional: some libraries and collectors prefer one, and some authors offer a hardcover as a special edition of a series favorite. If you add one, it needs its own ISBN, a cover designed for the case or dust jacket, and its own printed proof.

### What about large print?

Some readers prefer large print editions, and libraries often stock them. A large print edition needs its own layout and ISBN, but it reuses the edited text, which makes it a reasonable addition for an established series with loyal readers.

## When does an audiobook make sense?

Audiobooks have grown into a major format, and series fiction suits listening well. A listener who enjoys one book often goes straight on to the next. But audio is the most expensive format to produce, so timing matters.

### Why audio usually comes later

- **Production costs are high.** Narration is commonly paid per finished hour of audio, and a full-length novel runs many hours. A common rule of thumb is roughly 9,000 words per finished hour.
- **Series need consistency.** Listeners expect the same narrator across a series, so you are committing to more than one book.
- **Demand helps you decide.** Ebook and print sales show whether a series has the readership to justify the investment.

### Common ways audiobooks get made

- **Pay upfront.** You hire a narrator or producer and keep the full audio royalty.
- **Royalty share.** A narrator takes a share of the audio royalties instead of, or in addition to, an upfront fee.
- **Licensing.** An audio publisher pays for production in exchange for the audio rights and pays you a royalty.
- **Author narration.** For memoir and some nonfiction, listeners may prefer to hear the author's own voice.

### Check your audio rights first

For backlist titles, audio rights are often handled separately. The original publisher may have licensed audio to another company, and that license can continue after your other rights revert. Confirm who holds the audio rights before you plan an edition. Our guide to [republishing your backlist](/blog/how-to-republish-your-backlist/) explains how to check each right.

## Which format should a backlist title start with?

For a reissued backlist book, the ebook is usually the place to start. Many older titles were first published before ebooks were common, so a new digital edition can reach readers who never saw the book on a shelf.

Your rights may decide for you. If your ebook rights are free but a print edition from the original publisher is still on sale, the ebook may be the only edition you can release for now. Our [submission guidelines](/submission-guidelines/) note that it is okay if some of your backlist titles are still in print as physical copies, and that we can help you bring them into the digital age.

For a series, release the early books first and keep a steady pace. A reader who starts with book one needs book two to be ready.

## Should you release every format at the same time?

There are good arguments both ways.

**Releasing together** concentrates your marketing. Every reader who hears about the book can buy it in the format they prefer, and reviews in one format support the others.

**Staggering releases** spreads out the cost and the work. You can learn from the first launch, fix any errors readers report, and use early sales to decide whether audio is worth it.

A common middle path is to release the ebook and paperback together, since they share the same edited text and cover art, and to add the audiobook later.

## How do you keep a series consistent across formats?

Readers recognize a series by its look and its labels, in every format.

- **Adapt one cover design.** The ebook uses the front cover alone, the paperback needs a full wrap with spine and back, and audiobook retailers typically use a square image. Plan the design so it works in all three shapes.
- **Use the same series name and numbering everywhere,** so retailers can group the books and readers can find book two in whichever format they prefer.
- **Keep the descriptions aligned.** Each edition can share the same core description, with format details added where they help.
- **Keep the same narrator across the series** if you produce audio, and credit the narrator the same way on every edition.
- **Link the editions.** Major retailers can usually connect the ebook, print, and audio editions of a book on one product page when the title, author, and series details match.

Consistency matters most for backlist titles, where older editions may still be listed under a different look. Clear, matching details help readers find the edition they want and skip the ones they already own.

## How do formats affect what you earn?

Each format carries its own royalty rate and costs. Ebooks have no printing cost, so the author's share of each sale can be strong even at a lower price. Paperbacks carry a printing cost on every copy. Audiobooks often sell at a higher price but have a large production cost to recover first. Subscription services pay differently again, based on borrowing or listening rather than individual sales.

Before you sign any agreement, ask which formats it covers and what royalty applies to each. Our guide to [book royalties](/blog/book-royalties-explained/) explains what to look for.

## Which format should you publish first? A quick guide

- **A genre fiction series, new or backlist:** ebook first, paperback alongside or soon after, audiobook once the series shows demand.
- **A single backlist title with reverted ebook rights:** ebook first.
- **A book you will sell at events or to a local audience:** paperback early, alongside the ebook.
- **Nonfiction tied to your speaking or teaching:** paperback and ebook together, and audio if your audience listens.
- **An author with a large existing audio audience:** consider releasing audio with the other formats.

> Start with the format that reaches your readers fastest, then add the formats that reach the rest.
