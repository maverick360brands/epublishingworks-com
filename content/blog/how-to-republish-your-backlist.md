**To republish your backlist, get written confirmation that your rights have reverted, gather a clean manuscript for every title, give each book new packaging, and then decide who will publish the new editions.**

Many established authors have a shelf of books that sold well once and then disappeared when the original publisher let them go out of print. Those books still have readers. Some of those readers have never heard of you, and some have been waiting years for the rest of a series they started.

This guide covers how to get your rights back, what you need before a book can be republished, how to make an older title feel current, and your options for publishing it again.

## Why is your backlist worth republishing?

A backlist is every book you have already published. For a career author, it is often the most valuable asset you own, for three reasons.

- **Readers binge series.** Mystery and romance readers in particular tend to discover an author through one book and then read everything else. Every title you bring back is one more book a new fan can buy next.
- **Older books never had today's tools.** A novel first released in print years ago may never have had an ebook edition, a series page at online retailers, or a description written for people shopping on a phone.
- **A new edition resets first impressions.** A cover that looked right on a bookstore shelf decades ago can look dated as a small thumbnail on a screen. Fresh packaging gives the same story a better chance.

One of our authors, David O. Stewart, was frustrated that the original publisher of his historical mystery trilogy "slapped boring covers on the books and stopped promotion way too early." In his words, "ePublishingWorks! produced high-quality covers and gave the series a second life."

## How do you get your rights back from the original publisher?

Rights rarely come back on their own. A book going out of print, or a publisher losing interest in it, usually does not change who holds the rights. In most cases you have to ask, and you have to ask the way your contract describes.

### Find the reversion clause

Start with the contract for each book. Look for a section titled reversion, termination, or out of print. It will spell out when you can request your rights back and how.

Pay close attention to how the contract defines "in print." Older contracts often tie it to whether an edition from the publisher is available for sale. Because ebooks and print-on-demand editions never run out of stock, some publishers argue that a book listed in those formats is still in print. Newer contracts often use a sales or income threshold instead, so a book that sells below a set level over a set period counts as out of print. Your clause decides which test applies.

### Send a written reversion request

Write a short, specific letter. Include:

- Your legal name and any pen name the book was published under
- The title, original publication date, and ISBN of each book
- The date of the contract and the clause you are relying on
- A clear request that the rights granted under the contract revert to you

Send it by the method the contract's notice clause requires, and keep a copy of everything you send and receive. If an agent negotiated the original deal, involve them. Reversion requests are part of an agent's job.

### Allow for the response period

Many contracts give the publisher a set period to respond to your request, and some let the publisher keep the rights by putting the book back on sale within that window. Put the deadline on your calendar and follow up if it passes without an answer.

### Get the reversion in writing

What you want at the end is a reversion letter that names each title, states which rights have reverted, and gives an effective date. File it with the original contract. Any publisher you work with next will want to see it.

### What if the publisher closed or won't respond?

Publishers merge, sell their lists, and shut down. If yours no longer exists, find out who acquired its contracts, since the rights often pass to the buyer. If a publisher simply ignores you, keep a paper trail and get advice. Writers' organizations such as the Authors Guild and Authors Alliance publish guidance on rights reversion, and an intellectual property attorney can review your contract.

### What about the 35-year termination right?

In the United States, authors can in many cases terminate a publishing grant made on or after January 1, 1978, during a five-year window that opens 35 years after the grant, under Section 203 of the Copyright Act. Notice must be served within a set period before the termination date and recorded with the U.S. Copyright Office. The rules are technical and do not apply to works made for hire, so talk to an attorney before relying on them. If you have books from the 1980s and early 1990s, it is worth knowing the option exists.

## Which rights do you actually have back?

A reversion letter may return some rights and not others. Before you plan a relaunch, make a simple checklist for each book.

| Right | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Print | Has the publisher stopped selling its print edition, and have print rights reverted? |
| Ebook | Were ebook rights part of the original grant, and are they included in the reversion? |
| Audio | Was an audiobook licensed to another company, and is that license still running? |
| Translation | Are any foreign-language editions still under license in other countries? |
| Film and other rights | Were any options or licenses granted, and when do they end? |

Licenses that the original publisher granted to other companies can continue until they expire, even after your main rights revert. Knowing exactly which formats are free tells you which editions you can release first. Our guide to [which format to publish first](/blog/ebook-vs-paperback-vs-audiobook/) can help with that decision.

## What do you need before you republish?

Once the rights are yours, gather the raw materials. This is where older titles most often hit snags, so start early.

### A clean, editable manuscript

The text is yours, but the publisher's typeset files usually are not. You need an editable file of the final published text, which may differ from the draft on your old computer after copyedits and proof corrections.

If all you have is a printed copy, the book can be scanned and converted to text. Scanning introduces errors, from broken words to misread punctuation, so plan for a careful proofread of every converted title.

### New cover art

Cover illustrations, photographs, and designs are usually licensed to the original publisher or owned by the artist. Assume you need a new cover unless you secure the right to reuse the old one. That is rarely a loss. A new cover is one of the clearest ways to show that a book belongs to today's market.

### New ISBNs and an updated copyright page

An ISBN identifies a specific edition from a specific publisher, so the new edition needs its own ISBN for each format that uses one. The copyright page should keep the original copyright date and can note that the book was previously published, along with the details of the new edition.

### Your publication history

Make a record of each book's original title, publisher, publication year, and place in any series. If a book appeared under a different title or pen name, note that too. You will use this record when you write descriptions and set the series order, and readers appreciate knowing that a book is a reissue before they buy it.

### Reviews and endorsements worth keeping

Short quotes from past trade reviews, and endorsements from other authors, can often be reused with attribution. Gather the best ones now. They help a reissued book show its track record.

## How do you give an older book a new life?

A reissue is a chance to present the book the way it deserved to be presented all along.

### Refresh the cover for today's readers

Cover conventions change. Historical romance, cozy mystery, and thrillers each have a look readers recognize at a glance, and that look shifts over time. A series should also look like a series, with consistent type, layout, and style across every book.

At ePublishingWorks, we test cover designs with focus groups to make sure a book grabs attention.

### Rewrite the book description

Back-cover copy written for a print edition years ago was often brief, or written in a style that reads as dated online. A new description should hook readers in the first lines, signal the genre clearly, and name the series and book number. Our guide on [how to write a book description that sells](/blog/how-to-write-a-book-description/) walks through it.

### Decide how much to revise

Most authors keep the story intact. Readers who loved the original often want the book they remember, and a heavy rewrite can turn a reissue into a different book. Common light-touch updates include:

- Correcting errors the original edition missed
- Smoothing a few lines that have aged badly
- Updating the front and back matter, including your list of other titles
- Adding an author's note or a reading-order list for the series

If you do revise substantially, say so in the description so returning readers know what to expect.

### Organize the series

If your backlist includes connected books, settle on a series name and reading order and use them consistently on every cover, title page, and retailer listing. Books that were once split between publishers can finally sit together under one series name. Box sets, which bundle several books, give new readers an easy way in. Several of our authors' mystery series are available as box sets.

### Think about pricing and timing

Reissues can sit alongside new releases in a single career plan. Releasing older titles on a steady schedule keeps your name in front of readers, and pricing across a series can encourage people to start with book one. Jillian David, one of our authors, writes that our team "rebranded my series and adjusted pricing, helping me achieve Amazon and Barnes & Noble bestseller rankings in multiple categories."

## Should you self-publish your backlist or work with a publisher?

Both paths can work. The right one depends on how much of the business you want to run yourself.

### Self-publishing your backlist

Self-publishing gives you full control over covers, prices, and timing. It also gives you the whole workload: file conversion, proofreading, cover design, descriptions, retailer accounts, metadata, and promotion, for every title. With a large backlist, that can mean months of production work before you write another word.

T.M. Simmons, an ePublishingWorks author, describes trying that route first: "When I got my rights back on a bunch of my books, I attempted to go the self-publishing route. It soon got tiring, since I wanted to write, not do all the formatting, promotion, etc."

### Questions to ask any publisher about your backlist

Whoever you are considering, ask:

1. Which rights and formats does the agreement cover?
2. How long does the agreement last, and how do the rights come back to you if it ends?
3. How are royalties calculated, and how often are they paid?
4. Who designs the covers and writes the descriptions, and how much say do you have?
5. What happens to editions already on sale if you part ways?

Question 2 matters most. You went to the trouble of getting your rights back once, so make sure any new agreement explains clearly how you would get them back again. Our guide to [book royalties](/blog/book-royalties-explained/) covers the money questions in more detail.

> The books you have already written can keep earning, but only if readers can find them.

## What does a backlist relaunch look like, step by step?

Here is a practical order of operations:

1. **List every title** with its original publisher, contract date, and current status.
2. **Request reversion** for each book that qualifies, and track the responses.
3. **File every reversion letter** with its original contract.
4. **Gather clean manuscripts,** scanning and proofreading where needed.
5. **Map your series,** including reading order and series names.
6. **Choose your path:** self-publishing, or a publisher that takes backlist titles.
7. **Refresh the packaging:** covers, descriptions, and front and back matter.
8. **Release on a schedule** so that each book helps sell the next.

You do not need every title ready before you start. Many authors begin with the first books in a series and keep going as more rights come back.
