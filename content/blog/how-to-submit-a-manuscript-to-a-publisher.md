**To submit a manuscript to a publisher, read its submission guidelines first, confirm it publishes your kind of book, prepare exactly what it asks for, and send it the way it asks you to.**

That sounds obvious, but many submissions fall short on the basics: the wrong genre, a missing piece, an unfinished manuscript, or instructions ignored. Getting those right means your book is judged on its merits.

This guide covers what to prepare, how to present it, and what happens after you hit submit, with notes on how the process works at ePublishingWorks.

## Do you need a literary agent to submit to a publisher?

It depends on the publisher. Most of the largest traditional publishers accept submissions mainly through literary agents. Many independent publishers, small presses, and digital-first publishers accept submissions directly from authors.

If you are aiming for a large traditional house, your first step is usually querying agents. If you are aiming for an independent publisher, you can often submit directly. ePublishingWorks accepts submissions directly through our [submission form](/submit-your-manuscript/).

## How do you know a publisher is the right fit?

Before you prepare anything, make sure the publisher wants what you write. Sending a thriller to a publisher that only handles romance wastes everyone's time.

Check four things:

- **Genres.** Does the publisher handle your genre and sub-genre?
- **What it is seeking now.** Some publishers list current priorities, such as series or previously published titles.
- **Its books.** Look at its covers, descriptions, and authors. Would your book sit comfortably beside them?
- **Its terms.** Royalties, rights, and services vary widely. Our guide to [book royalties](/blog/book-royalties-explained/) explains what to compare.

## What should you prepare before you submit?

Gather everything before you open the form or write the email. Scrambling halfway through a submission leads to mistakes.

### A finished, clean manuscript

For fiction, most publishers expect a complete manuscript. A finished book shows you can deliver, and an editor needs to see that the ending works.

Clean means more than free of typos:

- Use a standard, readable font at a normal size, with consistent paragraph formatting.
- Mark chapter breaks clearly and start each chapter on a new page.
- Remove tracked changes, comments, and highlighted notes to yourself.
- Put the title, your name, and your contact details on the first page.
- Save the file in a format the publisher accepts and name it clearly, such as Title-AuthorName.docx.

ePublishingWorks accepts manuscripts as .pdf, .doc, .docx, or .zip files, up to 20MB.

### A short description of your book or series

Nearly every publisher wants a quick summary of what the book is about. Practice saying it in a few sentences: who the main character is, what they want, what stands in the way, and what kind of book it is.

Our submission form asks, "What is your book or series about?" and allows up to 350 characters, which is about two or three sentences. Here is a made-up example of the kind of answer that works:

> A widowed bookshop owner in 1920s Boston turns amateur sleuth when her late husband's business partner is found dead in her stockroom. Book one of a planned six-book cozy historical mystery series.

The skills that go into this summary are the same ones you need for retail copy, which we cover in [how to write a book description that sells](/blog/how-to-write-a-book-description/).

### A synopsis, if the publisher asks for one

A synopsis is not the same as a description. It summarizes the whole plot, including the ending, so an editor can judge the story's structure. Keep it to the length the publisher requests, write it in the present tense, and focus on the main character's arc and the key turning points rather than every subplot.

### Your series plan

If your book is part of a series, say so, and say how many books are finished, in progress, or planned. Publishers of genre fiction often care as much about the series as about the single book, because readers who like book one go on to buy the rest. At ePublishingWorks, series of three or more titles are preferred, and we look for a plan for six or more titles from new authors.

### Your publication history and rights status

List your published books with their titles, publishers, and years. For previously published titles, have your reversion letters ready to show that the rights are yours. When authors contact us, we ask them to include a list of past titles, if any, along with their genre and sub-genre of interest. If you are still working on getting rights back, see [how to republish your backlist after your rights revert](/blog/how-to-republish-your-backlist/).

### Your editing history

Publishers want to know what state the manuscript is in. Our form asks whether your book has already been edited, and if so, by whom. Be honest. An editor should know what they are looking at.

At ePublishingWorks, we proofread manuscripts, but we have limited capacity to edit them. For our authors, we can connect you with editors who can help.

### A short author bio

Write a few sentences about your writing life: past publications, series, awards, and any experience that connects you to your books. Third person usually reads best, and specific details beat adjectives. "Author of eight Regency romances and a three-book mystery series" tells an editor more than "an acclaimed, passionate storyteller."

### Your reasons for looking for a publisher

Our form also asks whether you are seeking to solve a problem with a new publisher, and what interests you most about working with us. The options include higher royalties, retaining book ownership, the ability to focus on writing, professional guidance, alignment and fit, and our genre list. A clear, specific answer helps us understand what you need.

## How do you write a cover letter or query?

Some publishers use a submission form. Others ask for an email with a cover letter or query. Even when a form handles the structure, it helps to have the same pieces written out.

A strong cover letter or query includes:

1. **A one-line hook** that captures the book's premise.
2. **The essentials:** title, genre and sub-genre, word count, and whether the book is part of a series.
3. **A short pitch,** one or two paragraphs on the main character, the conflict, and the stakes.
4. **Comparable authors or titles,** if you can name a few that share your book's readers.
5. **A brief bio** focused on your writing: past publications, awards, and relevant experience.

Keep it to one page. Address it to a person by name if the guidelines give one, and follow any instructions about what to paste into the email and what to attach.

## What mistakes get submissions passed over?

Editors see the same problems again and again. Avoid these:

- **Ignoring the guidelines.** If a publisher asks for a particular file type or set of materials, send exactly that.
- **Submitting the wrong genre.** A great book in a genre the publisher does not handle is still a no.
- **Sending a first draft.** Revise before you submit. You may get only one look.
- **Burying the premise.** If an editor cannot tell what the book is about from your summary, the manuscript starts at a disadvantage.
- **Leaving out the series.** If more books exist or are planned, say so. It can change the decision.
- **Being vague about rights.** For previously published books, unclear rights can stall an otherwise good fit.
- **Sending a generic pitch everywhere.** A note that shows you know what the publisher publishes stands out.

## Can you submit to more than one publisher at once?

Often, yes, but check first. Some publishers accept simultaneous submissions and some do not, and their guidelines usually say which. If you do submit to several at once:

- Keep a simple log of where you sent the book, when, and what each publisher asked for.
- Tailor each submission to the publisher's genres and guidelines rather than sending one generic package.
- If you receive an offer, tell the other publishers still considering the book, and give them a chance to respond before you decide.
- Wait out each publisher's stated response time before you follow up, and keep any follow-up short.

Be careful with previously published titles. Do not submit a book as available until you hold its rights, even if you expect them back soon. If a reversion is still in progress, say so plainly so the publisher knows where things stand.

## What happens after you submit?

Most publishers acknowledge a submission, review it, and respond within the time their guidelines state. Use the wait well by working on your next book.

If a publisher is interested, expect a conversation about your book, your series plans, and the terms of an agreement. At ePublishingWorks, the path has three steps: submit your manuscript for review and consultation, meet with us and get published, and earn more royalties as your book reaches more readers.

Good questions to bring to that conversation with any publisher include:

- Which of my books are you interested in, and in what order would you release them?
- What will you do for the covers, descriptions, and promotion?
- How are royalties calculated, and how often are they paid?
- What do you need from me, and by when?

Before you sign with anyone, read the agreement closely. Know which rights you are granting, how royalties are calculated and paid, and how you can end the agreement. If any term is unclear, ask, and consider having an attorney or agent review the contract.

If the answer is no, take any feedback seriously, check whether the publisher was truly the right fit, and keep going. A no from one publisher says little about how another will respond.
