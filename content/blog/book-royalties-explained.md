**A book royalty is the author's share of each sale, and a royalty rate only means something once you know what it is a percentage of.**

Two offers can quote very different percentages and pay similar amounts, or quote similar percentages and pay very different amounts. The difference is in the details: list price or net receipts, advance or no advance, which formats are covered, and how often you get paid.

This guide explains how royalties work in traditional publishing, in self-publishing, and with an author-first publisher like ePublishingWorks, and what to ask before you sign anything.

## What is a book royalty?

A royalty is a payment to the author for each copy sold, calculated as a percentage. Three details decide what you actually earn:

- **The rate.** The percentage itself, which often differs by format.
- **The base.** What the percentage is applied to: the book's list price, or the money the publisher actually receives from the retailer.
- **The schedule.** How often sales are reported and paid, and whether any money is held back.

### List price vs. net receipts

**List price** is the retail price of the book. **Net receipts**, often shortened to "net," is what the publisher receives after the retailer or distributor takes its share.

Here is a simple illustration with round, made-up numbers. Imagine an ebook priced at $5.00, sold through a retailer that passes 70% of the price on to the publisher. The publisher receives $3.50.

- A royalty of 10% of list price pays the author $0.50.
- A royalty of 25% of net receipts pays the author about $0.88.
- A royalty of 50% of net receipts pays the author $1.75.

The numbers are invented, but the principle is real. The same percentage can mean very different money depending on the base, so always ask which one a contract uses.

## How do traditional publishing royalties work?

A traditional publisher licenses the right to publish your book, pays for production, and pays you a royalty on each sale. Terms vary by publisher, format, and author, but some ranges are widely documented.

### Typical traditional royalty rates

- **Hardcover:** commonly 10% of list price, often rising to 12.5% and then 15% once sales pass agreed thresholds.
- **Trade paperback:** commonly around 7.5% of list price.
- **Mass-market paperback:** commonly in the single digits as a percentage of list price.
- **Ebook:** commonly 25% of net receipts at large publishers.
- **Audiobook:** usually a percentage of net receipts, with terms that vary widely.

These are starting points, not rules. Smaller presses, deep-discount sales, book club editions, and special sales often pay differently, and every contract is negotiated on its own.

### Advances and earning out

Many traditional deals include an advance, money paid before publication, usually in installments. It is an advance against royalties: your royalties first repay it. You receive no further royalty payments until your book "earns out," meaning the royalties credited to you exceed the advance.

Many books never earn out. In that case the advance is usually yours to keep, but it also means the advance is the only money the book pays you.

### Agent commission

Most deals with large traditional publishers go through a literary agent, who typically takes 15% of the author's domestic earnings, and often more on foreign sales. That commission comes out of both the advance and the royalties.

### Subsidiary rights

A traditional contract often covers more than the editions the publisher produces itself. Subsidiary rights include audio, translation, book club, large print, and film or television. When the publisher licenses one of these to another company, the income is usually split between author and publisher, with percentages that vary by right and by contract. Your share is typically credited to your royalty account, so it also counts toward earning out the advance. Before signing, check which subsidiary rights the publisher controls, what the splits are, and whether rights it never uses can come back to you.

### Statements and reserves

Traditional publishers commonly issue royalty statements once or twice a year, and payment often arrives months after the reporting period closes. Many also hold back a "reserve against returns," because bookstores can return unsold print copies for credit.

### What you get in return

The publisher covers editing, design, printing, and distribution, and an established house brings relationships with booksellers. For some books and some authors, that investment and reach are worth the smaller share.

## How do self-publishing royalties work?

When you self-publish, you are the publisher. Retail platforms pay you a share of each sale directly, and you keep what is left after your costs.

### Typical platform rates

Rates and rules change, so check each platform's current terms. As a widely cited example, Amazon's Kindle Direct Publishing pays a 70% royalty on ebooks priced within a set range (currently $2.99 to $9.99 in major markets, less a delivery fee based on file size) and 35% outside that range. Print-on-demand paperbacks earn a percentage of the list price minus the printing cost.

Other retailers and distribution services set their own rates. Aggregators that deliver your book to many stores at once take a share for the service.

### The costs you carry

Higher rates come with higher costs. A self-published author pays for editing, proofreading, cover design, formatting, and any advertising, usually before the book earns anything. You also spend your own time on uploads, metadata, pricing, and promotion, book after book.

### The exclusivity trade-off

Many self-published authors enroll their ebooks in Kindle Unlimited, Amazon's subscription service, which pays authors from a shared fund based on pages read. To enroll through KDP Select, the ebook must be exclusive to Amazon for each 90-day enrollment period, so it cannot be sold through other ebook retailers during that time.

## How are subscription and library reads paid?

Not every read is a sale, and these reads pay differently from a royalty on a single copy.

- **Subscription services.** Kindle Unlimited pays from a shared monthly fund, divided according to how many pages of each book subscribers read. Audiobook subscription services have their own models. What you earn depends on how much of the book people actually read, not on its list price.
- **Library lending.** Libraries generally license ebooks and audiobooks from publishers and distributors rather than buying them at retail, under terms that vary by platform. A library read can introduce a reader to a series they go on to buy.

For series authors, both can be valuable ways for readers to discover you. Ask any publisher which subscription and library channels your books will reach, and how income from them appears on your statements.

## Why isn't the royalty rate the whole story?

What a book earns you is, roughly, the royalty per copy times the number of copies sold, over the whole life of the book. Each part of that matters.

### Sales volume

A high rate on a book few people find pays little. Distribution, covers, descriptions, pricing, and promotion drive volume on every path. A strong [book description](/blog/how-to-write-a-book-description/) is one of the cheapest improvements you can make.

### Your writing time

Every hour spent formatting files and running ads is an hour not spent writing. Our view is that readers want a steady stream of new books, and that consistent releases of two to three books a year are crucial for staying relevant. For many genre authors, the biggest financial lever is simply publishing more books, especially in a series.

### Your backlist

Royalties on older books add up. A series that keeps selling for years can out-earn a single big launch. If some of your books are out of print, our guide to [republishing your backlist](/blog/how-to-republish-your-backlist/) explains how to bring them back.

### Payment timing

Royalties paid more often are easier to plan around. Ask how often statements are issued and how soon payment follows the end of each period.

## What should you ask before signing a royalty agreement?

Bring this list to any contract conversation:

1. What is the royalty rate for each format: ebook, print, and audio?
2. Is each rate based on list price or net receipts?
3. Do rates change at sales thresholds, or for discounted and special sales?
4. Is there an advance, and how is it paid?
5. How often are statements issued and royalties paid?
6. Is any money held back as a reserve, and for how long?
7. Are any costs charged against your royalties?
8. Which rights does the agreement cover, and for how long?
9. How do the rights revert to you if sales slow or the agreement ends?
10. Can you review the sales records if a statement looks wrong?

If a publisher can answer all ten clearly, you are in a good position to compare offers. If it can't, that tells you something too.

> The best royalty is one you can understand, verify, and collect.

## Which path pays best for you?

There is no universal answer, but a few patterns hold.

- **If a large publisher offers a meaningful advance and wide bookstore reach,** traditional publishing can make sense, especially for a book with broad commercial appeal.
- **If you enjoy running a business and can fund production,** self-publishing gives you the highest per-sale share and complete control.
- **If you want a high royalty share without running the business yourself,** an author-first publisher can be the better fit, particularly if you write series and have several completed books.
